The Amortization Game II: Can Taste Be Learned?

How much intelligence can be compressed into intuition, and how much must be reconstructed through search?

Hypothesis

The most capable systems will repeatedly move computation between intuition and search. A policy guides search, search produces experience, and experience builds a better policy.

The missing intuition

You can understand a great deal of physics and still fail to solve a particular physics problem.

You may know the relevant equations. You may follow the solution once someone shows it to you. But you did not notice the right symmetry, choose the right approximation, or represent the problem in the right way.

What was missing?

It may not have been knowledge. It may have been taste: the ability to recognize which direction is promising before you have fully explored it.

This makes me think the boundary between System 1 and System 2 is more important than I initially realized.

System 1 is fast and intuitive. System 2 is slow and deliberative. For models, the closest equivalents might be an amortized policy stored in the weights and computation performed through search at runtime.

But the interesting question is not simply how much of each a domain requires.

Can successful deliberation eventually become intuition?

Search and amortization

Suppose a model encounters a difficult problem.

Its weights provide an initial policy: a prior over plausible actions, representations, and answers. A harness then lets it search by trying alternatives, running tools, collecting evidence, and inspecting consequences. An evaluator helps it decide which branches were useful.

If those decisions and outcomes are recorded, they can become training data. Some of the work that previously required runtime search might then be compressed into the next version of the policy.

Policy prior → Runtime search → Evaluation → Traces → Improved policy

Search discovers useful structure. Training amortizes some of that structure into the policy. The improved policy then guides the next search.

AlphaGo-like systems are a canonical example. A policy narrows the space of plausible moves, while tree search examines their consequences. Search remains useful because the game tree is too large to absorb completely into a fixed policy. But the policy makes search much more productive than exploring from scratch.

This suggests one interpretation of intuition:

System 1 may be the reusable residue of previous System 2 computation.

I do not think this is literally true of every intuition. But it is a useful model of how a capable system might move computation between training time and test time.

A domain does not have one fixed ratio

I initially wanted to describe a domain by its ratio of System 1 to System 2 thinking.

But that ratio is not fixed.

It depends on the particular task, the capability of the policy, the available tools, the compute budget, and the quality of the feedback. What requires extensive search today may become nearly automatic after training. A new or unusual task can push the same system back toward deliberation.

It is more useful to give each task an amortization profile with four properties.

  1. Policy sufficiency: How far can the learned policy get without explicit search?
  2. Search leverage: How much does additional runtime computation improve the result?
  3. Evaluator quality: Can the system recognize improvement when it finds it?
  4. Distillability: Can successful search be compressed into a policy and reused?

A search trace is only valuable for training if it reveals something generalizable. Sometimes it teaches a reusable strategy. Sometimes it records a solution that works only for one instance. Sometimes the trace is not a faithful account of why the decision succeeded at all.

These variables are not inherent constants of an entire field. Physics is too broad. Solving an olympiad problem, fitting an experimental model, and choosing a frontier research direction occupy very different places.

Taste acts before verification

A verifier judges a candidate after it exists. Taste influences which candidates are generated in the first place.

Taste can mean:

  • choosing a useful representation;
  • noticing the assumption worth questioning;
  • selecting which evidence to gather;
  • recognizing an analogy that may transfer;
  • abandoning a technically valid but unimportant direction;
  • deciding when further search is unlikely to help.

This is why generating more reasoning traces does not automatically produce better judgment.

A polished trace may be a rationalization written after the important intuitive leap. It may omit rejected alternatives, uncertainty, and failed experiments. It may show the successful path without exposing why that path was selected over thousands of plausible ones.

If we want to learn taste, the valuable data may not be the final chain of reasoning. It may be the trajectory surrounding it:

  • What alternatives were available?
  • Which one did the expert inspect first?
  • What did they reject?
  • Which observation changed their direction?
  • When did they decide to stop?
  • Did the judgment remain valuable after more evidence arrived?
A trace of conclusions teaches less than a trace of decisions under uncertainty.

Human teachability is not model distillability

A skill being difficult for humans to learn does not mean it cannot be amortized into a model. A skill humans acquire intuitively does not mean a model can recover it from text.

Humans develop judgment through practice, embodiment, imitation, social feedback, and repeated exposure to consequences. Much of this learning is difficult to articulate. Experts may make excellent decisions without being able to produce a faithful explanation of how they made them.

Models encounter a different learning environment. They may identify statistical regularities across more examples than a human could experience, while lacking feedback that humans receive naturally from interacting with the world.

This gives us at least three separate questions:

  1. Can the judgment be learned?
  2. Can an expert explain or demonstrate the judgment?
  3. Can the learning system observe the feedback that made it useful?

Physics makes the distinction visible. A student can memorize equations without developing physical intuition. An experienced physicist may recognize the right approximation immediately but struggle to explain the years of examples that produced that recognition. A frontier researcher may possess excellent taste and still pursue a hypothesis that turns out to be wrong because the feedback is sparse and delayed.

The problem is not simply transferring a reasoning trace. It is reconstructing the learning environment that created the judgment.

Harnesses change the amortization profile

A task's position on the map is not permanent.

A harness can expose state, actions, and feedback that were previously hidden. It can turn an opaque final answer into a sequence of inspectable decisions. It can make tools available, preserve failed branches, and connect intermediate choices to later outcomes.

This can improve search leverage because the system can explore the domain more deliberately. It can improve evaluator quality by breaking one ungradable judgment into smaller, locally testable decisions. It can improve distillability by recording state, action, and outcome traces rather than only final answers.

But a harness cannot manufacture ground truth.

It may expose the wrong state, reward the wrong behavior, or formalize an expert's current ontology so rigidly that the system cannot discover a better one. Making a domain searchable is itself a design decision about what counts as an action and what counts as progress.

This is the tension I see in both chip design and debate.

Chip design contains many strong local evaluators, but they operate at different stages and levels of fidelity. A candidate can improve an early proxy while degrading the final system. The opportunity is to connect bounded search loops without pretending that any single metric captures the whole chip.

Debate has the opposite problem. Producing alternative arguments is easy. Evaluating whether a discussion discovered something true, or merely generated more persuasive language, is much harder.

With Crux, the question I am interested in is not simply whether agents can search across arguments. It is whether the search process can expose the judgments required to distinguish a productive disagreement from an endless one.

The weak-verification frontier

In strongly verifiable domains, the amortization loop is relatively legible:

Search more. Evaluate outcomes. Train on the useful trajectories. Search better.

In weakly verifiable domains, every step becomes questionable.

Did the evaluator reward truth or persuasiveness? Did the successful trajectory contain a reusable insight or benefit from luck? Did training capture better judgment or merely teach the policy to imitate the evaluator's preferences?

This may be the boundary of the amortization game.

The hardest domains are not necessarily those with the largest search spaces. They may be those in which we cannot tell which parts of a successful search deserve to become intuition.

The central question is therefore no longer only:

How much should a system learn during training, and how much should it search at runtime?

It is also:

Which discoveries can be safely amortized, and what evidence would tell us that the resulting intuition is genuine taste rather than compressed evaluator bias?

Perhaps intelligence is not located in either System 1 or System 2.

Perhaps it is the ability to move computation between them: to use intuition to guide search, search to produce experience, and experience to build better intuition, without losing the capacity to search again when that intuition stops working.
