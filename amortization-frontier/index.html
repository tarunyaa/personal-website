The Amortization Frontier

The boundary between what a model has learned to do in its weights and what it must still search over at runtime.

Hypothesis

The optimal train/test-time allocation is a property of the domain, and can be predicted from,

Combinatorial search complexity × Verification advantage
Definitions

Combinatorial search complexity: What the trained policy can’t already resolve.

Verification advantage: How cheaply candidates can be evaluated.

Premise

Most domains can be formulated as a ‘game’.

Hypothesis

Harnesses can change that split. They are not just tool integrations. They can make a domain searchable by exposing state, actions, and feedback.

Two domains I work on

I arrived at this through two domains I work on: chip design and debate.

Chip design has an enormous combinatorial structure but strong verifiers: routing, timing, congestion, etc. At NVIDIA, the issue was that there was no ontology to build this search structure. Once I built that, I was able to let my agent rip overnight and produce usable floorplans for my team.

Debate has a similarly large search space but much weaker verification: for open-ended research questions, truth is often delayed, expensive, or contested rather than immediately checkable. With Crux, I’m externalizing the search over competing arguments to identify the smallest disagreement that changes the conclusion, and collecting the human judgments that could eventually teach models better research taste.

So. For every domain

  1. Can you formulate it as a ‘game’?
  2. What is search space size and verifier availability
  3. Let it rip: self play and make it better

We need to get to letting it rip. And this is the amortization frontier I’m interested in.

Can we make search productive in weakly verifiable domains and then amortize what it discovers back into the policy?

Why externalize search?

The benefit of external search is not merely more inference-time compute. It converts otherwise invisible reasoning into a sequence of observable decisions: which branch to explore, what evidence to seek, when to abandon a hypothesis, and when to stop. Internal reasoning leaves us with a final answer. External search produces state-action-outcome traces. If humans can evaluate those decisions, the traces can train the next search policy.

How can a harness help weak verification?

A harness cannot manufacture ground truth, but it can decompose an ungradable final judgment into more locally testable decisions. In Crux, we may not know the correct answer to a policy question, but we can test whether evidence supports a claim, whether a proposed hinge is genuinely disputed, and whether changing it would alter the conclusion. The wager is that enough reliable local feedback can make search productive even when the final answer remains unverifiable.

The falsifiable claim

External search should outperform a direct model call only when its intermediate evaluators can rank branches better than the model’s implicit judgment. Otherwise, the harness adds ceremony rather than intelligence.

My first Crux ablation demonstrated exactly that: the single agent beat the more expensive search harness.

Things I’m thinking about

Jev

The recent release of Jev has confirmed my suspicions.

Jev demonstrates the value of making a decision problem explicit. It maps a legible state and a bounded set of typed questions into fast probabilistic judgments.

External search creates exactly this interface: it turns an implicit chain of thought into inspectable states, branches, and candidate actions. Once reasoning is externalized this way, a cheaper model can repeatedly decide which branch to expand, abandon, or investigate, without asking a full generative model to reconstruct the entire problem each time.

Search generates the trajectories; repeated decisions over those trajectories can eventually be amortized into a faster policy.

Humans&

Persimmon is closer to the weak-verification regime. There is no single correct human response, so Humans& trains against an adaptive discriminator and evaluates whether behavior matches the distribution of real human interactions over time.

The horizon is what forces that choice. Pure next-token prediction drifts as error compounds over a long conversation, and a fixed reward stops carrying signal once the model’s own distribution moves under it. An adaptive discriminator recalibrates as the model improves. It is a verifier trained alongside the thing it verifies.

The numbers show how hard the regime is. Persimmon fools an LLM judge about 20% of the time, where 50% would mean indistinguishable, against 1.2% for the base model it started from. At 80 turns it holds coherence in 60.7% of conversations against 87.3% for humans. GPT 6 scores 98.4% on that same measure, which is the tell: being too coherent is its own way of failing to look human. You cannot score this domain on a scale where more is better.

The analogous question for Crux is: what evaluator could distinguish genuine research taste?

AlphaGo

The closest, most popular analogue is AlphaGo: training compresses search into a policy network but at inference time, the system still performs MCTS because the search space is so large.

Motivation

I work on AI for GPU floorplans, alongside building Crux. Both projects make me ask whether the bottleneck is the model or the environment we give it.

The Bitter Lesson makes me wary of hand-built ontologies. Are we encoding an expert’s preferred answers, or providing a representation in which search and learning can discover better ones? That is the tension I want to investigate.
