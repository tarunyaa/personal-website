The Amortization Frontier

Every domain has an optimal split between what you train into the weights and what you search for at test time. I think the split is predictable — and movable.

The views and opinions expressed in this article are my own and do not represent those of my employer.

Hypothesis

The optimal train/test-time allocation is a property of the domain, and can be predicted from two terms:

Combinatorial search complexity
What the trained policy can’t already resolve.

Verification advantage
How cheaply candidates can be evaluated.
The two terms, at more length

Combinatorial search complexity is not the raw size of the space. It’s the residual — the part of the space the system still has to walk because the policy can’t pattern-match its way to the answer. A domain can be astronomically large and have almost no residual search: there are more grammatical English sentences than chess positions, but a good language model doesn’t search over them. The residual is what’s left after amortization, which means it shrinks as models improve. It is a property of the domain and the current policy, not the domain alone.

Verification advantage is the gap between the cost of producing a candidate and the cost of judging it. When that gap is wide, search is nearly free — you can afford to be wrong a thousand times, because being wrong is cheap to notice. When it’s narrow or nonexistent, every sample you draw costs about as much to evaluate as it did to generate, and search stops paying for itself.

The product is what predicts the architecture. Both high, and you keep a search loop at test time more or less forever. Both low, and you amortize everything into weights and ship a single forward pass. The interesting cases are the corners where one term is large and the other isn’t.

Premise: most domains can be formulated as a game

A game needs four things: a state you can observe, a set of actions you can enumerate, a transition rule, and some signal about how you did. That’s a low bar, and more domains clear it than people assume. What usually blocks it isn’t that a domain is mysterious or irreducibly human. It’s that nobody has built the representation in which the state is observable and the actions are enumerable.

Once a domain has that shape, self-play is on the table. And self-play is the only mechanism we know of that produces capability nobody demonstrated first.

Hypothesis: harnesses move the split

A harness gets described as tool integration — give the model a shell, a browser, an API, some function calls. I think that undersells what it does. A harness is what makes a domain searchable. It exposes state, enumerates actions, and returns feedback. It decides whether the model gets to look at a position or has to guess at one.

Which means the two terms above aren’t fixed properties of the world. They’re properties of the world plus the interface you built over it. A domain with a huge space and no exposed state looks weakly verifiable, and you can spend years believing that, when the truth is that you never built the thing that would have done the verifying.

This is the part of the framework I’m least sure about and most interested in. If harnesses move domains around the map, then the map isn’t a taxonomy of how hard things are. It’s a snapshot of how much engineering has been done.

Two domains I work on

Chip design has enormous combinatorial structure and genuinely strong verifiers. Routing, timing, congestion — all computable, all fast relative to the cost of producing a floorplan. On paper it sits in the best corner of the map.

At NVIDIA the bottleneck turned out to be neither the verifier nor the model. There was no ontology to search over: no clean object model of what a floorplan is, what moves are legal, what a partial solution looks like, how to say that two candidates differ. The verifiers existed but the agent had nothing to hand them. Once I built that representation, I could let the agent rip overnight and come back to floorplans my team could actually use. Nothing about the model changed. The representation changed.

Debate has a comparably large space and weak verification. For open-ended research questions there is no ground truth to check against — that’s most of what makes them research questions. You can’t compile an argument and you can’t run its tests.

With Crux I’m trying to externalize the search: define a structured disagreement space so that where exactly do these two people diverge becomes an object you can enumerate over rather than a thing you have taste about. If that works, a model can be trained to have research taste inside the structure, the same way a policy network gets trained to have positional taste inside chess. The bet is that you can manufacture enough verification signal out of structure to make search productive even where no oracle exists.

I don’t know yet whether that bet is right. It’s the reason I care about this framework at all.

So, for every domain

  1. Can you formulate it as a game?
  2. What is the search space size, and what verifiers are actually available to the agent?
  3. Let it rip — self-play, and make it better.

Most of the field’s effort right now sits before step one: making the model better at guessing. I think more of it belongs at step one. We need to get to letting it rip.

Can we make search productive in weakly verifiable domains, and then amortize what it discovers back into the policy?

That’s the amortization frontier. Not the question of whether to train or to search — that one resolves itself once you know the two numbers — but the question of whether the numbers themselves can be engineered.

Things I’m thinking about

What Jev changed, and what it didn’t

TypeSafe released Jev three days ago, and it sharpened something I’d been circling. Jev doesn’t generate text. It takes unstructured state plus a set of typed questions and returns decisions with calibrated probabilities, non-autoregressively, in 70–500ms rather than seconds. The training method they call RLCD — reinforcement learning for calibrated decisions — optimizes for probabilities that are epistemically honest rather than for outputs a human would rate highly.

The number everyone quoted was the speed. I think the speed is the least interesting part. Verification advantage is a ratio, and Jev is a two-orders-of-magnitude cut to the denominator. A cheap, calibrated, typed scorer over an enumerated action space is a value function in everything but name. That moves domains rightward on the map without anyone building a new oracle — the oracle was always affordable in principle and too expensive to put in a loop.

Their Wikiracing demo is the tell. Hundreds to thousands of link choices per step, no dense reward, and what makes the search tractable is simply that scoring the options got cheap enough to do at every node. That is the AlphaGo shape arriving in a domain nobody had bothered to formulate as a game.

But I want to be honest about the limit: Jev is deliberately a short-horizon model. System One. Nothing about it addresses credit assignment across long horizons, which is the actual obstacle in the weak-verification regime. It doesn’t move debate. It moves every domain where the actions were always enumerable and evaluation was merely too expensive to loop on — which is a lot of domains, just not mine.

The open question I’d want answered: does a scorer trained for calibration stay calibrated off-distribution? Because a search loop’s entire job is to go off-distribution. A verifier that’s honest on the training manifold and quietly confident outside it is exactly the failure mode that makes search turn into adversarial search against your own metric.

Background: the AlphaGo analogue

The closest popular analogue is AlphaGo. Training compresses search into a policy network, but at inference the system still runs MCTS, because the space is too large for the policy to cover alone.

The lesson most people took from it was “self-play works.” The lesson I take is that the policy and the search were never competitors. The policy’s job was to make the search cheaper; the search’s job was to generate better training data for the policy. The split between them wasn’t a design preference — it settled where the domain’s two numbers put it, and it would have settled somewhere else in a different domain.

Which is the whole argument here, just restricted to a domain where both numbers happened to be enormous and someone had already built the harness for us. Go came pre-formulated. Almost nothing else does.

Motivation, and the thing that worries me

I work on AI for GPU floorplans, alongside building Crux. Both projects keep pushing me to the same question: is the bottleneck the model, or the environment we give it?

The Bitter Lesson makes me wary of hand-built ontologies. Every ontology is a claim about what matters, and the history of the field is mostly a history of those claims being wrong in ways their authors couldn’t see. So the question I keep asking about my own work: are we encoding an expert’s preferred answers, or providing a representation in which search and learning can discover better ones?

The floorplan ontology I built could honestly be either. It produced floorplans my team used, which is evidence of something, but “an expert’s preferences, executed faster” also produces usable floorplans. The test I haven’t run is whether the agent ever returns something that violates what I believed when I wrote the representation — and is right. Until a harness surprises its author, you can’t tell which kind you built.

That’s the tension I want to investigate.
